Public services still run on 1959 code.

This observatory maps the old technology behind UK public services, from programming languages and mainframes to phone lines, radio networks, devices and encryption: where they still run, who can maintain them, how they drive technical debt, and how government is moving off them. Every figure links to its public source. Our own judgements are labelled as such.

Cost

What legacy costs

Legacy costs money in seven ways: savings missed, a premium for running old technology, the share of budgets it absorbs, contracts extended to keep it alive, programmes to replace it, incidents it causes, and the price of scarce skills. No published figure adds these up, and we do not add them either: they overlap and cover different years and scopes.

Reading these figures

  • UK IT figures are from BCS's analysis of the ONS Labour Force Survey and cover all IT specialist jobs, public and private. Civil service figures cover the Digital and Data profession only. The two are different groups measured in different years (2023 to 2025).
  • Civil service shares exclude suppressed small counts. Ethnicity shares are of staff who declared an ethnicity; nearly a quarter of digital and data staff did not.
  • Our civil service pay gaps compare median salaries for each group. They are not adjusted for grade, so they show where pay differs, not why.
  • Nothing here measures the people who work on legacy code itself. We found no source that does, and say so rather than estimate it.

Beyond central government

Skills and talent

Who can still maintain it

No UK government dataset counts staff by programming language. What follows is the best available evidence: audit findings, live job adverts and industry surveys. Read the surveys as indicators, not a census.

Do skills shortages cause technical debt?

The evidence shows the two reinforce each other. A shortage makes change slower and dearer, so systems are left alone, so the code grows older and the pool shrinks further.

An age-inclusive approach to legacy skills

Retirement is a real risk to legacy knowledge. It is a planning fact about the workforce, not a judgement about any group of people, and the response must stay fair to people of every age.

Skills needed to refactor or replace legacy code

Moving off a legacy language takes two skill sets working together: people who can read the old system, and people who can build and run the new one. Role names follow the Government Digital and Data Profession Capability Framework. Analysis

Migration and decommissioning

How legacy code is moved

The umbrella term is application modernisation. Moving code from one language to another is code conversion or translation. Suppliers on the government's G-Cloud framework describe six routes, known as the 6 Rs.
